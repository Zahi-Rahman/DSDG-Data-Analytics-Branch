# Mini-Project 1 — What "Good" Looks Like

This isn't used for a formal grade — it's shared with members alongside the brief so nobody is guessing what's being evaluated, and it's what officers use to give real feedback during Q&A.

| Dimension | Developing | Solid | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The question** | Given/obvious, answerable by sorting a column | Specific and non-obvious | Specific, non-obvious, and the group can explain *why* it matters |
| **Feature engineering** | No new column, or one that just relabels existing data | One meaningful engineered column | Engineered column directly drives the central finding |
| **Analytical thinking** | Reports a pattern with no discussion of alternative explanations | Names at least one alternative explanation | Actively tests or rules out an alternative explanation using the data |
| **Limitations** | Not mentioned | Mentioned briefly | Specific, and the group explains how it would change their confidence |
| **Dashboard** | Visuals present, but unlabeled or hard to read cold | Clear, labeled, and readable without narration | Readable cold *and* the design choices (color, layout) support the claim |
| **Communication** | Claim unclear or buried | Claim stated clearly | Claim stated clearly in the first 30 seconds, with the strongest evidence led with, not saved for last |

A genuinely strong mini-project doesn't need to check every "Strong" box — this is a rubric for feedback, not a checklist to grade against. The single most important row is **analytical thinking**: a technically polished dashboard built around an obvious, unexamined claim is a weaker project than a rougher one built around a real question the group had to work to answer.
